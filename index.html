How the scores are calculated

Sector score = Quality × 40% + Growth × 35% + Timing × 25%. As of 1 Sep 2026, all three are built from real inputs: Timing comes from each sector's actual index PE vs its own historical average (cheaper vs history scores higher). Growth comes from a real, recently published industry growth forecast (e.g. NASSCOM for IT, CRISIL for capital goods, India Ratings for bank credit). Quality is the hardest to reduce to a clean number — it's built from well-established, real sector characteristics (regulatory barriers, brand moats, cyclicality, disruption risk) run through the same scoring bands, not looked up from a single source. Treat Quality with more caution than the other two.

Company score combines four sub-scores:

  • Profitability (35%) — average of (ROE ÷ 30 × 100) and (ROCE ÷ 30 × 100), each capped at 100.
  • Growth (30%) — (3-year revenue growth ÷ 25 × 100), capped at 100.
  • Balance sheet (20%) — 100 − (debt/equity ÷ 1.5 × 100), capped at 0–100.
  • Valuation (15%) — 50 − (PE premium or discount vs. sector, in percentage points).

Final score = Profitability × 0.35 + Growth × 0.30 + Balance sheet × 0.20 + Valuation × 0.15, rounded to a whole number.

Bands: 70+ strong candidate · 50–69 watchlist · below 50 reject.

Banks don't fit that formula — ROCE and debt/equity aren't meaningful when deposits are 30-40x equity by design. Banks use a variant instead: Profitability from ROE alone (35%), Growth from revenue growth (30%), Asset quality from Net NPA % instead of debt/equity (20%), Valuation unchanged (15%).

A few real findings worth noting: TCS and HCL Technologies land in "watchlist" despite excellent profitability, because mega-cap revenue growth of 6-9% scores low against this formula's growth benchmark. Both specialty chemicals picks land in "reject" — the sector's 3-year sales growth has genuinely stalled industry-wide, not a formula quirk. And Larsen & Toubro's higher leverage pulls it well below the nearly debt-free Cummins India, even though both are respected engineering names.

Indo Tech Transformers was found by actually running a growth/quality screen against Screener.in's full database — not by picking a recognizable name. It scores highest on the site (93), but 80% of its promoters' shares are pledged, a serious risk this formula doesn't check for. That gap is exactly why a forensic/red-flag layer comes after the quant screen, not instead of it — see its flag in the table instead of a plain verdict.

That same screen surfaced Ceinsys Tech, G M Breweries, and Jaro Institute — three companies most people wouldn't recognize, all scoring well on real numbers. Two other screen results were deliberately left out: Jindal Poly Investment isn't a real operating business (it's an investment holding company whose "growth" is just investment-gain accounting noise), and Vivid Electromech's numbers are too unstable post-IPO to trust yet. A screen finds candidates — it doesn't replace judgment on whether they're comparable at all.

Jaro Institute is the first company in "Education," a sector with no ranking row yet and no peer group — its valuation score defaults to neutral until there's another education company to compare it against. And once a small, very different business like G M Breweries joins a broad bucket like "FMCG," the sector's average PE swings hard, which distorts everyone else's valuation score too — a real limitation of comparing companies only against whoever else happens to already be listed under the same label.

Real sector scores shifted the ranking in telling ways. Pharmaceuticals fell from mid-pack to last: its 9% growth forecast is genuinely healthy, but the sector trades 23% above its own historical valuation — a "priced for perfection" signal the placeholder version never showed. FMCG rose from last to second: modest growth, but strong brand-moat quality and a real valuation discount to its own history. Capital goods dropped from 2nd to 4th once its true valuation (fairly priced, not cheap) replaced an overly optimistic guess.
